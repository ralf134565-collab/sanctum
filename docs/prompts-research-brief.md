# Scientific and UX Foundation for Mood-Linked Prompts — Sanctum

This brief outlines the clinical and psychological research backing the prompt system of **Sanctum** (PocketReflect). Sanctum acts as a local, non-judgmental evening reflection journal. The goal of the prompt system is to feel "in place" — a quiet companion that senses the user's emotional state without performing unsolicited therapy, diagnostic labeling, or toxic positivity.

---

## 1. Key Psychological Paradigms & Evening Integration

### 1.1 Affective Labeling
*   **Scientific Backing:** Lieberman et al. (*Putting Feelings Into Words*, 2007; *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*) demonstrated that translating emotional states into words (affective labeling) reduces amygdala reactivity and increases activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (rvlPFC). In other words, labeling an emotion dampens its visceral impact.
*   **Sanctum Application:** Evening prompts must not ask the user to analyze or justify their feelings immediately. Instead, they invite them to name or locate the feeling in their immediate experience (e.g., *"What feeling today did not find words?"*). This facilitates emotional cooling at the end of the day.

### 1.2 Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)
*   **Scientific Backing:** Hayes et al. (1999) emphasizes cognitive defusion (observing thoughts as passing events rather than absolute facts or commands), acceptance (allowing negative emotions to exist without struggle), and value-directed actions.
*   **Sanctum Application:** Prompts for negative states (anxiety, irritation, sadness) encourage defusion and acceptance (e.g., *"If anxiety were a signal, what is it guarding?"*). Prompts for focused states link actions to personal values (e.g., *"Where did you choose your own way today?"*).

### 1.3 Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) — Mild Restructuring
*   **Scientific Backing:** Beck et al. Cognitive restructuring involves identifying automatic negative thoughts and cognitive distortions (like catastrophizing) and gently widening the cognitive field.
*   **Sanctum Application:** Rather than rigorous cognitive challenging (which feels cold or academic), Sanctum uses gentle evening decatastrophizing and perspective-taking (e.g., *"What is the worst that happens if this situation is left unresolved until tomorrow?"*). This helps close open cognitive loops.

### 1.4 Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)
*   **Scientific Backing:** Kabat-Zinn (1990) defines mindfulness as paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, non-judgmentally.
*   **Sanctum Application:** Focuses heavily on somatic observation and anchoring (e.g., *"Where in your body did tension live today, and where did you feel support?"*). This guides the user out of rumination ("mind-talk") and back into somatic reality.

### 1.5 Savoring
*   **Scientific Backing:** Bryant & Veroff (*Savoring: A New Model of Positive Experience*, 2007) detail savoring as the deliberate cognitive process of attending to, appreciating, and enhancing positive experiences.
*   **Sanctum Application:** Used specifically for positive states (calm, joy, gratitude). Prompts help anchor, replay, and share positive emotions to build psychological resilience (e.g., *"What fleeting detail brought a genuine smile to your face today?"*).

### 1.6 Self-Compassion
*   **Scientific Backing:** Kristin Neff (2003) conceptualizes self-compassion as having three components: self-kindness, common humanity, and mindfulness on painful thoughts.
*   **Sanctum Application:** On difficult, low-energy days (tiredness, overwhelm, sadness), prompts validate effort and soften self-judgment (e.g., *"What did you tell yourself more harshly today than you would a close friend?"*).

### 1.7 Behavioral Activation (Mild) & Cognitive Offloading
*   **Scientific Backing:** Martell et al. (Behavioral Activation for Depression) emphasizes small, values-driven steps rather than macro-planning. Under the Zeigarnik Effect, uncompleted tasks remain active in working memory, causing sleep disruptions and stress.
*   **Sanctum Application:** Prompts encourage tiny, immediate boundary adjustments or offloading tasks onto the screen (e.g., *"What is one small task you can cross off your to-do list for tomorrow?"*).

## 2. Best Practices for Evening Reflection Prompts

### What to Do (The Sanctum Tone):
*   **Keep it open-ended:** End with exactly one question mark (`?`).
*   **Maintain evening horizon:** Anchor the reflection in *this specific evening*, helping the user transition to rest (e.g., "on this evening", "put down until tomorrow").
*   **Use the respectful RU "Вы" (capitalized/respectful):** Sanctum addresses the user gently and respectfully, with absolute lack of preachy or overly familiar slang.
*   **Validate first:** For irritation, sadness, tiredness, and anxiety, validate the feeling rather than encouraging the user to "fix it" or look on the bright side.

### What to Avoid (Strict Constraints):
*   **No clinical diagnostic terms:** Avoid terms like "depression", "panic attack", "social anxiety", or "trauma".
*   **No toxic positivity or gaslighting:** Do not ask an anxious/sad user to *"focus on the positive"* or *"what made you happy anyway?"*. This invalidates real distress.
*   **No productivity coaching or checklists:** Avoid questions like *"Did you reach your goals?"*, *"How productive were you?"*, or *"What can you do to work harder tomorrow?"*.
*   **No cognitive overload:** Keep questions shorter than **120 characters in RU** (aiming for single-line display in UI) and syntactically simple.
